# Methodology draft

**Version:** 0.1 draft for review  
**Purpose:** A practical workflow for analyzing public observations about suspected online fraud without overstating what the evidence supports.

This is a standalone methodology draft, not a validated detector, legal standard, or finding about any real subject. It makes no accuracy, efficacy, or novelty claim. Before public release, the author should review provenance, attribution, and rights for every included idea and asset. No license is chosen.

## 1. Scope and question

Start with a narrow, answerable question about a public object or claim, such as whether two publicly visible storefronts appear to share an operator. Record the reason for looking, the objects in scope, the observation period, and explicit exclusions. Use public, passive sources only. Collect the minimum relevant information.

Do not turn a broad suspicion into an open-ended personal dossier. If the question cannot be investigated safely or with appropriate authority, stop and document the limitation.

## 2. Keep analytic layers distinct

Use separate fields or sections for:

1. **Observation:** What was directly seen or retrieved, in what source, and at what time.
2. **Inference:** What that observation may imply, with the reasoning stated.
3. **Hypothesis:** A candidate explanation that needs testing, including alternatives.
4. **Confidence:** A qualitative assessment of the specific claim, based on evidence quality, relevance, freshness, and independence.
5. **Human disposition:** A reviewer’s current status, rationale, and date. This is a human decision, not a model output.

Do not silently promote an inference into an observation or a hypothesis into a conclusion. Unknown means unknown; missing evidence is not evidence of absence.

## 3. Capture and assess sources

For each material observation, record a source identifier, source type, original URL or reference, observation time (with time zone), capture method, and any transformation such as transcription or normalization. Keep a short excerpt or description sufficient to locate the fact; avoid retaining unnecessary personal data or raw content.

Assess each source for:

- **Provenance:** Can another reviewer locate and understand the source?
- **Directness:** Is it a first-party record, an independent report, a repost, or an assertion?
- **Freshness:** When was the fact observed, and does it describe current or historical state?
- **Integrity and limits:** Could the page have changed, been copied, or been misattributed?
- **Relevance:** Does it bear on the exact claim, or only on general context?

A search snippet, accusation, or AI summary is a lead. Verify material claims against the underlying source where possible. When verification is unavailable, label the limitation.

## 4. Resolve entities and relationships cautiously

State exactly which entities or artifacts are being compared and which attributes match or conflict. Distinguish a domain, a page, a business name, a person, a payment identity, and an operator; they are not interchangeable.

Represent a relationship as a claim with its own supporting observations, provenance, time, alternative explanations, and reviewer status. Look for independent corroboration. Multiple pages repeating one original report are one source lineage, not multiple independent confirmations.

Shared hosting, registration services, templates, stock images, certificates, analytics tags, generic wording, or similar names are weak contextual signals on their own. A relationship does not transfer the classification of one entity to another. Historical association does not prove current control.

## 5. Test alternatives and disconfirmation

For each important hypothesis, write at least one plausible benign alternative and identify what evidence could distinguish it. Consider copied content, authorized syndication, common vendors, stale pages, unrelated parties using shared infrastructure, mistaken identity, and source error where relevant.

Actively seek disconfirming information within the safe scope. Record negative results as bounded observations: say what was checked, where, when, and what the check could not establish. Do not treat a failure to find contrary evidence as confirmation.

## 6. Qualify confidence

Use qualitative, claim-specific terms such as **low**, **moderate**, or **high**, and explain the basis. Consider directness, source quality, relevance, freshness, independence, counter-evidence, and unresolved alternatives. Confidence in one observation does not automatically transfer to an entity-linkage or wrongdoing claim.

Do not present a heuristic, risk index, or model score as a probability unless it has been properly defined, calibrated, and validated for that use. This draft supplies no calibrated score or fraud verdict.

## 7. Human review and reporting

A reviewer should be able to trace each material statement back to a source and see the counter-evidence and limitations. The reviewer records a disposition, rationale, date, and unresolved questions. Machine-generated suggestions, if any, remain separate and advisory; they never change human disposition automatically.

A concise report should state:

- the narrow question, scope, and observation window;
- what is directly observed versus inferred;
- the strongest supporting and contrary evidence;
- source independence, freshness, and coverage limits;
- benign alternatives and unresolved questions;
- the human disposition and its date;
- safe, proportionate next steps, if any.

Use precise language such as “the observed pages share a footer link” or “this may warrant further review.” Avoid declaring that an identifiable person or organization is fraudulent or criminal unless an authorized process and sufficient independently reviewed evidence support that conclusion. This methodology itself is not such a process.

## 8. Correction, retention, and safety

Preserve enough provenance to revisit material claims, and correct or withdraw conclusions when sources change or errors are found. Set a purpose-based retention period; minimize personal information and restrict access to sensitive records. Do not publish case material or allegations without separate review and authorization.

This workflow excludes authentication, contacting targets or third parties, scanning, evasion, form submission, credential testing, transactions, and intervention. It does not override law, platform rules, organizational policy, or required legal/ethical review.
